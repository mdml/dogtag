# Drift Mapping

The ordinary map of a coast is a line: the surveyor decides where the water ends and the land begins, fixes that decision at some agreed state of the tide, and draws it. Everything about the method is honest and everything about the result is a fiction, because the line does not exist for more than the minute in which it was measured. Drift mapping is the opposite bet. Instead of asking where the edge *is*, it asks what the edge *collects* — and lets the accumulation draw the map.

The practice, as I have pieced it together from Krantz's notebook essays and two seasons of trying it myself, has three movements.

**First, the walk.** You walk the same stretch at the same state of tide, repeatedly, over months. Not to measure anything — to notice what the water has left. Wrack lines, of course, but also the sorted bands within them: the cork and bottle-cap band, the weed band, the fine black band of coal dust that still comes ashore a century after the last collier. Each band is a record of a different transport process, which means each band is drawn by a different sea. A single beach on a single morning is already a stack of maps.

**Second, the ledger.** Every walk gets a strip entry: date, tide, wind from memory or the harbour log, and then the bands in order from the water up, with widths paced rather than taped. The discipline that matters is refusing to interpret while recording. The morning you decide in the field that the coal band "means" a southeasterly is the morning you stop seeing the bands that contradict you. Interpretation happens at the desk, in a different notebook, at least a week later. This rule I take directly from the winterbourne chapter of [[A Field Guide to Vanishing Rivers]], where the author makes the same point about dry channels: the field walker records the gravel, the desk worker argues about the flood.

**Third, the overlay.** After enough strips accumulate, you draw the beach not as a line but as a ribbon of probability: here the weed band sits four walks out of five; here the cork band wanders six paces between spring and neap; here, and this is the part that justifies all the walking, a band appears that no single walk would have shown you, because it only assembles across time — the slow eastward migration of everything buoyant, which is the longshore drift made visible without a single instrument.

What you get at the end is not a chart anyone could navigate by, and that is the point. A drift map is a portrait of the *processes* at an edge, in the way the conventional map is a portrait of a single decision about it. The two do not compete; they answer different questions. The chart says where you may safely put a keel. The drift map says what the coast is doing, and roughly how fast, and in which direction it will disagree with the chart next year.

Objections I have collected, mostly from myself:

- *It is not reproducible.* Two walkers pace differently, notice differently, name the bands differently. True, and the same is true of any field notebook ever kept; the ledger discipline narrows the gap but does not close it. The answer is that a drift map is signed work, like a drawing, and should be read the way a drawing is read.
- *It is just beachcombing with a diary.* Also true, in the sense that surveying is just walking with a stick. The method is in the repetition and the refusal to interpret early, not in any single observation.
- *The processes change faster than the map can record them.* Sometimes — a single storm can rewrite a shingle beach overnight. But the storm shows up in the ledger as a discontinuity, and the discontinuities turn out to be the most legible thing in the whole record. The map absorbs its own revisions in a way the charted line never can.

For the atlas, drift mapping is the organising idea of the coastal plates: each plate pairs the surveyed line with the ribbon, engraved as a stipple field, so the viewer sees both the decision and the argument against it. Whether that reads to anyone who has not spent two years walking the same beach is an open question, and probably the central risk of the whole project.

The thing I keep circling back to: every archive is a wrack line. The vault this note sits in collects what the days wash up, sorts it into bands, and the shape of the collection over time says more than any single entry. I did not expect a cartographic method to double as a description of note-keeping, but here we are, and it is why this note is filed under method and not under whimsy.
